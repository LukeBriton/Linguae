#English #Phonology #Phonetics

## Phonology


- Great Vowel Shift and trisyllabic laxing

- L-vocalization
	since [Early Modern English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Modern_English "Early Modern English"), before [coronal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coronal_consonant "Coronal consonant") or [velar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velar_consonant "Velar consonant") [consonants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consonant "Consonant"), or at the end of a word or morpheme.
	- -al-: /al/ became /awl/ and then /ɑul/ (exception: shall, shalt)
	- -ol-: /ɔl/ became /ɔwl/ and then /ɔul/
	-> The [Great Vowel Shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Vowel_Shift "Great Vowel Shift"): /ɑu/ becoming the monophthong /ɔː/, and /ɔu/ raising to /oʊ/.
	- In *-alk* and *-olk* words, the /l/ subsequently disappeared entirely in most accents, some have more than one pronunciation that retains the /l/ sound:
		caulk/calk, falcon, yolk
	- fault and vault underwent L-vocalization in Old French, but had it restored by Early Modern English
	- The loss of /l/ in words spelt with _-alf_, _-alm_, _-alve_ and _-olm_ did not involve L-vocalization in the same sense, but rather the elision of the consonant and usually the [compensatory lengthening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compensatory_lengthening "Compensatory lengthening") of the vowel.

	- no reduction of /hw/
	- reduction of /hl/, /hr/ and /hn/, with the loss of the initial /h/ in Middle English.
	- no reduction of /hj/ to /j/

- y-cluster reductions
	- no yod-dropping after /n/, /t/, /d/, /z/ and /θ/
		- yod-dropping after 
			- /ʃ, tʃ, dʒ/
			- /j/
			- /r/
			- stop+/l/
			- (Some) /s/ and /l/

- reduction of /wr/ and /wl/

- reduction of /mb/ and /mn/ to /m/
